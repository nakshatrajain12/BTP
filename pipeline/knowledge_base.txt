Startup India Seed Fund Scheme (SISFS)  
Purpose & Objectives:  SISFS was launched in 2021 with a ₹945 Crore corpus to provide critical 
early -stage financial support to startups for proof of concept, prototype development, product 
trials, market entry, and commercialization. It bridges the funding gap at idea and prot otype stage, 
before angel/VC investment is available.  
Eligibility:  The startup must be DPIIT -recognized, incorporated less than 2 years before 
application. It should have a business idea to develop a product/service with market fit, viable 
commercialization, and scalability. The product/service should be technology -drive n. Indian 
promoters must own ≥51% of equity, and the startup should not have received over ₹10 lakh in 
funding from any other central/state scheme. (Certain sectors like social impact, waste 
management, agriculture, biotech, etc. are prioritise d). 
Support Offered:  Grant  – up to ₹20 lakh for validation of Proof of Concept, prototype 
development, or product trials (disbursed in milestones). Debt/Convertible Funding  – up to ₹50 
lakh for market entry, commercialization or scaling up, disbursed as debt or convertible debentures 
through incubators. The debt comes with an interest ≤ current repo rate, tenure up to 5 years with 
optional 12 -month moratorium. Funding is pro vided through accredited incubators , not directly 
to startups, and cannot be used for cr eating physical infrastructure.  
Application Procedure:  Applications are accepted online on the Startup India Seed Fund portal 
(seedfund.startupindia.gov.in) on a rolling basis. A startup can apply to up to three incubators of 
its choice. Incubators have experts committees (ISMCs) to evaluate submissions on cr iteria like 
problem -solution fit, innovation, team strength, and market potential. Shortlisted startups pitch to 
the committee, and funding decisions are made typically within 45 days of application. Required 
documents include incorpo ration certificate, pitch deck, etc.  There are no fees for applying.  
Nodal Agency:  DPIIT is the policymaking owner, and the scheme is implemented through the 
selected partner incubators that disburse funds and mentor startups.  
Operational Status:  Active.  As of Jan 2026, the ₹945 crore corpus has been approved to 215+ 
incubators to support early -stage startups under SISFS. Earlier Government reporting showed that 
by end‑2024 the scheme had approved 213 incubators, funded 2,622 startups and disbursed 
₹467.7 5 crore.  
Official Reference: Startup India Seed Fund Scheme portal: https://seedfund.startupindia.gov.in/ |

Fund of Funds for Startups (FFS)

Purpose & Objectives:  Launched in 2016 (under Startup India), FFS is a ₹10,000 crore corpus 
aimed at increasing availability of domestic venture capital for Indian startups. Uniquely, FFS does 
not invest directly in startups  but operates as a fund -of-funds : it invests in SEBI -registered 
Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs) – specifically Venture Capital Funds (VCFs) – which in turn 
invest in startups. This leverages professional fund managers to identify and support high -growth 
startups, and crowds in privat e investment alongsid e government money. Each FFS -backed fund 
must invest at least twice the amount of FFS contribution into startups, ensuring a multiplier effect.  
Eligibility:  For AIFs:  Must be Category I or II AIFs registered with SEBI, with a focus on 
investing in startups as per DPIIT’s definition. They should have a strong track record and the 
ability to raise co -investment – FFS typically contributes up to 10% of an AIF’s corpus, so  the 
fund manager must secure the rest. Certain sectors (like real estate, NBFC lending, etc.) are 
generally excluded from FFS support. For Startups:  There is no direct application to FFS by 
startups; they must meet the criteria of in dividual partner VC funds. Generally, the startup should 
be DPIIT -recognized (entity <10 years old, turnover under ₹100Cr, innovative/scalable business). 
Startups approach the VC funds (daughter funds) who manage the selection and investment 
decisions.  
Support Offered:  Indirect Equity Funding:  FFS commits capital to venture funds, which then 
invest equity or equity -linked instruments into startups. By December 2024, DPIIT had committed 
₹6,886 crore to SIDBI (the fund manager for FFS), and in turn, SIDBI’s VC arm had committed 
₹11,687 crore into  99 AIFs, which invested in 800+ startups. This structure channels government 
funds to startups via experienced investors, ensuring startups get not just capital but mentorship 
and network benefits from VCs.  
Application Procedure:  For AIFs:  Venture fund managers apply to SIDBI (the Small Industries 
Development Bank of India, which operates FFS) with their fund proposal. Applications involve 
presentations to SIDBI’s Venture Capital Investment Committee and due diligence. Funds are 
sanctioned based on meeting the scheme’s criteria. For Startups:  No direct application to FFS – 
startups seek funding from the VC/angel funds that have received FFS commitments. Startups 
should research which VC funds in their sector/g eography are backed by FFS and approach them 
as they would any other investor.  
Nodal Agency:  DPIIT is the policymaker/monitor for FFS, while SIDBI is the operating agency 
that manages the corpus, selects the VC funds, and administers investments.  
Operational Status:  Active.  As of 31 Dec 2025, SIDBI reported ₹11,808 crore committed to 153 
SEBI -registered AIFs; these AIFs have invested ₹22,942 crore in 1,270 startups. Union Budget 
2025 –26 also announced a fresh ₹10,000 crore Fund of Funds tranche to further deepen risk capital  
(deployment details per notified guidelines).  
Official Reference: SIDBI – Fund of Funds for Startups (FFS):

Credit Guarantee Scheme for Startups (CGSS)  
Purpose & Objectives:  CGSS was announced under the Startup India Action Plan to spur debt 
funding for startups by mitigating banks’ risk. It provides a credit guarantee  (backstop) by the 
government for loans extended to eligible startups, so that startups can obtain collateral -free 
borrowings from banks or financial institutions. The aim is to unlock debt finance for startups, 
which often struggle to get traditional loan s due to lack of assets.  
Eligibility:  DPIIT -recognized startups (meeting the official definition) are eligible to be covered 
under the guarantee.  Lenders (banks/NBFCs) can apply for guarantee coverage on loans up to the 
scheme’s limits when lending to these startups. The startup should be within 10 years of 
incorporation and meet other criteria as defined by the scheme guidelines (e.g. innovative n ature 
of business).  
Support Offered:  Guarantee Coverage:  Transaction -based guarantee: Up to 85% of the credit 
facility for loans up to ₹10 crore and 75% for loans above ₹10 crore (subject to the overall 
per‑borrower guarantee limit, revised up to ₹20 crore). Umbrella -based guarantee is also available 
for pooled  startup investments by eligible entities, covering actual losses up to a capped percentage 
of the pooled investment, as per the notified framework.  
Application Procedure:  Startups cannot directly apply to CGSS. Instead, they approach 
participating banks or financial institutions for loans. The lender, after sanctioning a loan to a 
startup, applies for the guarantee coverage under CGSS via the trust company’s portal. The pr ocess 
involves due diligence by the lender as per their norms (since credit risk is shared). Entrepreneurs 
are advised to inquire with banks whether they utilize CGSS for startup loans and then proceed 
accordingly.  The scheme’s detail s and application forms for lenders are available on the Startup 
India portal and NCGTC site.

Tax Incentives and Regulatory Support (Startup India)  
While not a “scheme” with an application form, it’s important to note DPIIT’s Startup India 
initiative provides tax and regulatory benefits  for recognized startups:  
• Income Tax Holiday:  Eligible startups (incorporated on/after 01 Apr 2016 and before 01 
Apr 2030, and approved by the Inter‑Ministerial Board where applicable) can claim a 100% 
tax deduction on profits for any 3 consecutive years out of the first 10 years, subject to 
Section 80‑IAC conditions.  
• Exemption on Capital Gains:  Section 54EE/54GB of IT Act provides tax exemption on 
capital gains invested in startups or funds of funds, encouraging funding into startups.  
• Easier Compliance:  Self-certification for labor and environmental laws for 3 -5 years to 
reduce regulatory burden.  
• IPR Support:  Fast-track patent examination and rebates on patent/trademark filing fees 
for DPIIT startups.  
• Public Procurement:  Relaxed norms (exemption from prior turnover/experience, etc.) to 
enable startups to bid for government tenders.  
These measures, implemented by various ministries (Finance, Labour, etc.), are part of the Startup 
India framework and significantly support new business creation by reducing costs and barriers.  
Nodal:  DPIIT (policy coordination) along with CBDT (for tax), etc. Status:  Active.

NIDHI – National Initiative for Developing and Harnessing Innovations  
Purpose & Objectives:  NIDHI is an integrated program supporting innovators and startups at 
different stages – from idea to market.  It aims to build an innovation -driven entrepreneurial 
ecosystem by providing financial and institutional support through a network of Technology 
Business Incubators (TBIs) across India.  NIDHI’s components include PRAYAS (Promoting 
and Accelerating Young Innovators) , NIDHI -EIR, NIDHI -Seed Support , NIDHI -TBI, and 
NIDHI -Accelerator , creating a pipeline of support:  
• NIDHI -PRAYAS (Idea to Prototype):  Provides young innovators with a grant to convert 
ideas into a prototype. Eligibility:  Individual innovators or early -stage startups (≤7 years 
old, revenue <₹25L) with a science/technology -based idea for a physical product 
prototype.  (Pure software, service, or academic research projects are excluded. ) Must be 
an Indian citizen (≥18 years); if employed or student, needs NOC.  Support:  Grant up to 
₹10 lakh for prototype development expenses (raw materials, fabrication, testing, patent

filing etc.) over up to 18 months.  Access to dedicated fabrication labs (“PRAYAS Shala”) 
at host incubators is also provided.  Application:  Apply to designated PRAYAS Centres 
(incubators) during calls; selection is merit -based by each center.  Nodal/Implementer:  
DST’s NSTEDB division, managed by a Program Management Unit (at SINE, IIT 
Bombay) and executed via selected incubators.  Status:  Active, with dozens of PRAYAS 
Centres across India supporting innovators annually.  
• NIDHI Entrepreneur -In-Residence (EIR):  Provides budding entrepreneurs (often 
graduating students) with a subsistence stipend to pursue their startup idea full -time for 12 
months instead of taking up jobs.  Eligibility:  Aspirants with a promising tech business idea 
who are affiliated with or recommended by a host incubator (EIR centre). Typically for 
first-generation entrepreneurs with no prior funding. Support:  A monthly fellowship 
(approximately ₹20,000 –30,000) for up to one year to cover living costs while  they develop 
a business plan or MVP. Application:  Apply to approved NIDHI -EIR incubators (called 
Program Execution Partners) when they open calls.  Nodal:  DST (through NSTEDB) with 
a selected incubator as the implementation partner. Status:  Active (multiple calls yearly).  
• NIDHI Seed Support Scheme (SSS):  Provides seed funding to early -stage startups to help 
them transition from prototype to market.  Eligibility:  Startups incubated at a DST -
approved TBIs, usually post -prototype and in need of funds for product development, 
testing, and go -to-market. Support:  Grant or equity seed funds up to ~₹25 lakh per startup 
(exact amount varies) to cover product development, team expansion, marketing, etc. 
Funds are disbursed through the incubator, which manages the seed investment. DST 
typically gives each incubator a corpus (e.g. ₹1 –2 Cr) to deploy as seed fund in multiple 
startups. Application:  Startups apply to their incubator when seed fund calls are 
announced. Selection is by the incubator’s investment committee. Status:  Active – 
numerous startups have benefitted via NIDHI seed support across the TBI network.  
• NIDHI Technology Business Incubators (TBI):  Supports establishment and operation of 
incubators in academic and R&D institutions to nurture startups.  Support:  DST provides 
up to ₹10 crore over 5 years to each approved TBI for infrastructure, equipment, 
operational expenses, and seed grants.  TBIs in turn provide physical space, labs, 
mentorship, and networking for startups. Application:  Institutions apply to DST NSTEDB 
for setting up a new TBI when calls are open (typically an expert committee evaluates 
propo sals). Status:  Active – over 150 TBIs established under NSTEDB, which have 
incubated thousands of startups.  
• NIDHI -Accelerator:  Helps accelerate growth -stage startups through a short -term cohort -
based mentorship and business development program. Support:  Grant funding to the 
accelerator organization (incubator or partner) up to ₹40 lakh per cohort to run an 
accelerator for selected startups.  Startups get mentoring, industry connects, and sometimes 
small grants or investor access. Status:  Active – DST has launched a few accelerator 
programs under NIDHI to boost promising startups in scaling up.  
Nodal Agency:  All NIDHI components are overseen by DST’s National Science & Technology 
Entrepreneurship Development Board (NSTEDB ). Implementation is through various host 
institutions (incubators, known as NIDHI centres for each component).

Technology Development Board (TDB)  
Purpose & Objectives:  TDB is a statutory body under DST that provides financial assistance to 
companies working on commercialization of innovative indigenous technologies . It bridges 
the gap between R&D and commercialization for tech projects. TDB primarily supports late-stage 
start -ups or tech enterprises  that have moved beyond proof -of-concept and need support for 
scale -up, manufacturing, or market launch.  
Eligibility:  Companies incorporated in India (typically start -ups, MSMEs, or innovative 
companies) with a near -market -ready product or technology of indigenous development. The 
project should involve development and deployment of technology that contributes to nationa l 
interest. The company should have a credible business plan and some skin in the game (promoters’ 
investment or other funding).  
Support Offered:  Soft loans  (generally at a simple interest rate of 5% per annum) or equity 
funding  for part of the project cost. TDB can fund up to 50% of the project cost (sometimes more 
in exceptional cases ). The funding usually ranges from ₹50 lakhs to ₹10+ crore depending on 
project scale. In some cases, TDB may also offer grants for particularly socially relevant projects 
or those involving academia. The support covers expenditures like pilot plant construc tion, testing 
and trials, intellectual proper ty protection, marketing launch, etc. The loan is repayable over years 
after a moratorium, from revenues generated.  
Application Procedure:  Companies apply directly to TDB with a detailed proposal. There is a 
prescribed application form on the TDB website. The evaluation process includes technical and 
financial appraisal by domain experts. TDB’s board approves projects based on uniqueness of 
technology, commercialization potential, national benefit, and credibility of the team. The process 
can involve presentations and due diligence on site. Calls are open year -round, and the Board 
meets periodically to sanction projects.  
Nodal Agency:  Technology Development Board (under DST) – it has its own Secretariat and 
Funds.  
Operational Status:  Active.  Since its inception (1990s), TDB has funded over 300 projects  
across sectors (like biotech, pharma, engineering, transport, energy) facilitating commercial launch 
of innovative products. Many startups have scaled into successful companies with TDB’s support 
(e.g., TDB supported the launch of India’s first electric c ar years ago, among other success stories). 
As of 2025, TDB continues to invite proposals, especially focusing on areas like clean energy, 
defense tech, healthcare, et c., aligned with national priorities.

Anusandhan National Research Foundation (ANRF) – Research, Development 
and Innovation (RDI) Scheme/Fund  
Purpose & Objectives: The RDI Scheme/Fund is designed to catalyse private -sector investment in 
R&D and innovation, improve translation of research to products, and support deep -tech 
commercialization (including through blended/concessional finance and equi ty-like instruments).  
Structure: The scheme is anchored by an ANRF Special Purpose Fund (SPF) that deploys capital 
through second -level fund managers/financial institutions (e.g., AIFs, DFIs, NBFCs and other 
eligible entities), enabling project finance and equity/equity -like su pport for innovation -led firms, 
including startups, as per notified guidelines.

TIDE 2.0 (Technology Incubation and Development of Entrepreneurs)  
Purpose & Objectives:  TIDE 2.0, launched in 2019, aims to promote tech entrepreneurship in 
areas of national concern by strengthening incubators  and supporting startups  in ICT domains.  
It focuses on startups leveraging emerging technologies (IoT, AI, blockchain, robotics, etc.) to 
solve problems in seven critical sectors: healthcare, education, agriculture, financial inclusion 
(fintech), infrastructure & transportation, environment & cleantech, and clean energy . The 
goal is to support ~2000 tech startups th rough a network of 51 incubators across India over five 
years,  with special emphasis on startups in smaller cities and addressing social challenges.  
Eligibility:  For Startups:  Early -stage tech startups working in one of the seven focus areas. 
Different programs under TIDE 2.0 target different stages – e.g., an “Ideation” grant for very early 
ideas vs. a “Product” grant for those with some prototype.  The startup should be incubated or 
willing to incubate at one of the TIDE -supported centers. For Incubators:  Institutions (universities, 
engineering colleges, R&D labs, etc.) with existing incubation capacity could apply to become 
TIDE centers. They were categorized into three tiers (Group 1/2/3) based on experience and 
capabilities, with Group 1 being more esta blished incubators.  
Support Offered:  
• To Incubators (TIDE centres):  Financial grants from MeitY to run and expand the 
incubator’s programs. Each of the 51 selected centers receives funding (quantum 
depending on their group) to cover operational expenses, to build or upgrade incubation 
facilities, and to provide financial support to startups.  For example, grants for procurement 
of lab equipment, or funds to offer seed grants and entrepreneur stipends.  
• To Startups (through TIDE incubators):  Financial assistance  in the form of small 
grants or stipends at various stages. For instance, an “Entrepreneur -in-Residence” stipend 
(₹7 lakh) for building product proof -of-concept.  These are indicative figures; actual 
amounts can vary by incubator program. Additionally, startups get incubation benefits : 
workspace, mentorship, business advice, access to investor networks, and linkages to other 
MeitY initiatives like domain -specific CoEs or IPR support.  TIDE incubators also connect 
startups with other government programs or seed funds when possible.  
Application Procedure:  Startups apply directly to the TIDE 2.0 incubator centers. Each TIDE 
incubator periodically opens calls for entrepreneurs to apply for specific programs (Ideation grants, 
etc.). The incubator evaluates proposals (usually a committee assesses the technology innovation, 
team, and social impact) and selects startups for support. The application process is advertised on 
the MeitY Startup Hub portal and the individual incubators’ webs ites. There is no fee; selection is 
merit -based. Incubat ors themselves were selected by MeitY via an open call in 2019 – that process 
is closed, but new centers may be added in future phases.  
Nodal Agency:  MeitY , implemented via the MeitY Startup Hub  which oversees coordination. 
Each participating incubator (TIDE center) executes the scheme at ground level.

SAMRIDH (Startup Accelerator for Product Innovation, Development and 
Growth)  
Purpose & Objectives:  SAMRIDH, launched in August 2021, is a MeitY scheme to spur the 
growth of startup accelerators and in turn accelerate product -stage startups. The objective is to 
scale up tech startups  by providing them with capital (through a matching investment model) and 
mentorship so they can grow, commercialize and get market traction.  It particularly focuses on 
startups that have a minimum viable product and some validation, and now need support for 
customer acquisition, business expansion and po ssibly pivoting for market needs.  
Eligibility:  For Startups:  Primarily IT/ICT startups  with a working product/solution and 
demonstrated proof -of-concept looking to scale.  They should ideally have some initial customers 
or pilots and be at growth stage (not idea stage). There is a focus on solutions that can have a broad 
impact and align with Digital India goals (including areas like healthtech, edtech, fintech, 
agriculture  tech, etc.). For Accelerators:  Existing startup accelerators (organizations that run 
fixed -term mentorship programs for  cohorts of startups) that have a track record or strong plan to 
nurture tech startups can apply to be partners under SAMRIDH.  They must bring industry expertise 
and invest capital alongside MeitY’s contribution.  
Support Offered:  SAMRIDH works by providing government co -funding to match private 
investment  in startups, routed through accelerators:  
• MeitY provides up to ₹40 lakh per startup  as seed investment/grant under the program.  
• This must be matched 1:1 by the accelerator or investor(s) network connected through the 
accelerator.  In essence, each selected startup can receive up to ₹80 lakh (half from 
government, half from private) to fuel its growth.  
• Additionally, the accelerators provide an intensive 3–6-month  acceleration program : 
mentoring in business scaling, product management, market access, investor readiness, etc. 
The cost of running these programs is partly funded by MeitY (the scheme supports the 
accelerator operations too).  
• The funding to startups is typically in the form of an investment (convertible debenture or 
equity) to ensure accountability and possibly returns that can make the accelerator 
sustainable.  
• Beyond funding, startups benefit from corporate connect, market linkages, and demo 
days for follow -on investment , facilitated by the accelerators.  
Application Procedure:  MeitY runs a selection process for accelerators. Accelerators apply with 
proposals; those approved are empanelled as “SAMRIDH accelerators”. Subsequently, these 
accelerators invite startups to apply to their programs (the call for startup applications is often 
listed on MSH and accelerator websites). Startups then go through the accelerator’s selection

(pitching, due diligence). If selected, a startup joins the accelerator program and becomes eligible 
for the matching investment support. Essentially:  
1. Accelerator selection:  By MeitY – (this was done for multiple cohorts; by end of 2022, 
several accelerators were onboarded).  
2. Startup selection:  By each MeitY -approved accelerator – they source and identify high -
potential startups for SAMRIDH support.  Application details and timelines are posted on 
the MeitY Startup Hub site. For example, well -known accelerators like IIT Madras’s HTIC, 
KIIT -TBI, etc., have been part of SAMRIDH.  
Nodal Agency:  MeitY , implemented through MeitY Startup Hub (MSH) . MSH coordinates 
with the partner accelerators to disburse funds and monitor progress.  
Operational Status:  Active.  As of 2024, SAMRIDH had onboarded 22 accelerators and aimed to 
support about 300 startups.  Many startups have already received funding and mentorship under 
the first cohorts, and new accelerator cohorts continue to be launched. The scheme is recognized 
as a key effort to push startups from initial traction to growth phase.  
Official References:  MeitY Startup Hub’s official SAMRIDH page contains  scheme guidelines,

GENESIS (Gen -Next Support for Innovative Startups)  
Purpose & Objectives:  Announced in 2023, GENESIS is an umbrella program by MeitY aimed 
at supporting startups in Tier II and III cities  and emerging tech domains. Its goal is to 
democratize the startup ecosystem by extending structured financial support and resources beyond 
the major startup hubs. GENESIS comprises multiple components covering the entrepreneurial 
journey:  
• Entrepreneur -in-Residence (EiR)  fellowships for idea -stage founders in smaller towns.  
• Prototype/Pilot stage grants  for startups building early products.  
• Scale -up funding (investments)  for promising startups ready to grow.  
• Support for Deep Tech startups  that require larger capital (up to ₹1 Cr) and longer 
gestation.  
Eligibility:  Startups and entrepreneurs located in Tier-II/III cities  (smaller cities and rural areas) 
are the primary target, though others can apply depending on component. Specific eligibilities:  
• EiR: individual innovators from smaller cities with a tech business idea.  
• Prototype grants: early startups or teams in emerging tech with proof -of-concept; likely 
requiring them to be incubated at local incubators or institutions.  
• Pilot/Investment: startups with a working prototype/MVP from non -metro regions, seeking 
to deploy pilots or scale operations. They may need to have matching investment lined up 
(since the scheme often does co -funding like SAMRIDH).

• Deep Tech: startups working on cutting -edge tech (AI, ML, semiconductor, cyber security, 
etc.) needing higher capital. Focus is also on inclusion – supporting underrepresented 
groups and sectors.  
Support Offered:  A multi -tier funding  structure:  
• EiR Fellowship:  on the order of ₹5 –10 lakh per innovator (to cover a year’s expenses 
while developing the idea or prototype).  
• Early -Stage (Ideation) Grant:  around ₹10 lakh to build prototypes or proof of concept 
for startups graduating from EiR or similar stage.  
• Pilot Stage Funding:  around ₹20 –₹25 lakh to help a startup conduct product pilots or 
market validation in real environment.  
• Scale/Investment Stage:  equity or grant support up to ₹50 lakh for startups with proven 
solutions to expand business operations (often requiring 1:1 match by private investors).  
• Deep Tech Research/Acceleration:  up to ₹1 crore support for deep -tech startups that 
typically have hardware or frontier tech which needs longer development (possibly also 
matching with private funds).  
Besides money, GENESIS would likely integrate mentorship networks, dedicated 
incubator/implementing agencies in smaller cities, and linkage to MeitY’s other resources (like 
MSH, CoEs). It’s designed to be an end-to-end pipeline : an entrepreneur could get an EiR 
fellowship, then a prototype grant, then pilot support, etc., as they progress.  
Application Procedure:  GENESIS is implemented via selected “Implementing Agencies” in 
various regions (could be incubators, accelerators, or state -supported hubs). These agencies float 
calls for applications under the GENESIS program components. Entrepreneurs/startups apply wit h 
details of their idea or business and the specific support needed. A selection committee at the 
implementing agency level evaluates and recommends support. Higher -value funding decisions 
may involve MeitY’s oversight or a central co mmittee approval. The calls and information are 
aggregated on the MeitY Startup Hub portal ( e.g., announcements for GENESIS EiR or pilot grants 
in specific states). The program was newly set up in late 2023, so processes are still being refined.  
Nodal Agency:  MeitY  (MSH). Execution is through partner incubators/organizations across 
India’s smaller cities.  
Operational Status:  Active (Newly Launched).  GENESIS received administrative approval in 
2023 and  launched initial components in late 2023 and early 2024. By 2025, it is rolling out across 
states – entrepreneurs are beginning to receive fellowships and grants. This scheme is a major push 
to broaden the startup base geographically and  is funded through the Digital India initiative.  
Official References:  MeitY’s GENESIS brochure and  approval document provide  details. MSH 
website lists GENESIS as a scheme with PDFs explaining each sub -program.

MSME Incubation Scheme (Incubation, Design & IPR under MSME Innovative 
Scheme)  
Purpose & Objectives:  The MSME “Incubation” scheme (part of the MSME Innovative Scheme) 
aims to encourage development of innovative products, processes or services by MSMEs and 
innovators. It provides financial assistance to business incubators to nurture new ideas to prototyp e 
stage, and to MSMEs/innovators to translate innovative ideas into viable businesses.  The broader 
goal is to promote adoption of latest technologies and spawn MSMEs that are knowledge -based 
and competitive.  
Eligibility:  There are two levels of eligibility:  
• Incubatee (Innovator/Startup):  Any individual or micro/MSME with an innovative idea 
can apply to be incubated under the scheme.  Individuals need an Aadhaar; MSMEs need 
Udyam registration. The idea should have significant innovation and commercial potential. 
Students or recent graduates with ideas can also apply (through a host institute).  
• Host Institute (Incubator):  Organizations like technical colleges, universities, 
engineering institutes, R&D labs, NGOs involved in entrepreneurship, industry 
associations, MSME Development Institutes, etc., can apply to register as approved 
incubators under the scheme.  They should have basic infrastructure to support startups. 
Each host institute is expected to form an Incubation Committee to evaluate and mentor 
ideas.  
Support Offered:  The scheme provides financial assistance in three main ways : 
1. Funding for Incubation of Ideas (for Incubatee):  Up to ₹15 lakh  per idea is given to the 
host incubator to support each approved project/ idea.  This is used to procure raw materials, 
equipment, tools, mentoring services, testing and trial etc. for developing the prototype. If 
the incubatee is an MSME, they need to contribute 15% of the project cost (for students or 
SC/ST/women, contribution may b e lower or zero ).

2. Procurement of Plant & Machinery (for Incubator):  Up to ₹1 crore  grant to the host 
incubator to set up or upgrade its facilities (labs, machinery, 3D printers, software, etc.) 
that will be used by the incubatees.  This is to ensure incubators have the necessary technical 
resources to support new product development.  
3. Seed Capital Support (to Incubator for Startups):  Additional grant up to ₹1 crore  per 
incubator to be used as seed funding for deserving incubatees to scale up from prototype 
to market.  This essentially allows the incubator to invest small seed amounts (in form of 
soft loan, equity, grant) into startups that graduate from the prototype stage, helping them 
commercialize.  
Other benefits include mentoring, access to the incubator’s infrastructure and network. Design and 
IPR: (Under the MSME Innovative umbrella, parallel support is also given for design 
improvements and for patent/trademark facilitation for MSMEs, though those are separate sub -
schemes.)  
Application Procedure:  
• For Host Institutes:  They must apply online on the MSME Ministry’s portal 
(my.msme.gov.in) to be empanelled as business incubators.  Proposals are reviewed by the 
Scheme Steering Committee.  
• For Innovators/Startups:  They should approach any approved host incubator with their 
business idea. The incubator’s internal committee does an initial evaluation and if 
recommended, the proposal is sent to the MSME Ministry for approval of funding. 
Applications can be made throug hout the year via the MIS portal of the scheme.  After 
approval, the incubator will receive the funds and disburse to the innovator/startup as 
needed for the project. The process involves a project report submission detailing the 
innovation, implementation plan, milestones, and budget. Each idea goes through scrutiny 
at multiple levels (incubator, then MSME Ministry’s board) before sanction.  
Nodal Agency:  Office of the Development Commissioner (MSME)  under the Ministry of 
MSME manages the scheme.  The grants are released to the host institutions. Implementing 
partners are the selected host incubators across India (over 150 incubators have been approved 
under this scheme).  
Operational Status:  Active.  The scheme (in various forms) has been running for several years; it 
was revamped under MSME Innovative Scheme in 2022 to combine Incubation, Design and IPR 
schemes. Dozens of innovative ideas have been supported – from agri drones to food processing 
tech – through MSME incubators. Many technical institutions, like IITs, NITs, state engineering 
colleges, etc., are participating as host incubators.  The government continues to sanction new ideas 
monthly.  
Official Reference:  Scheme guidelines are published on the MSME ministry website and  the

ASPIRE – Promotion of Innovation in Rural Industries and Entrepreneurship  
Purpose & Objectives:  ASPIRE  was launched in 2015 to promote entrepreneurship in rural 
areas, particularly in agriculture and allied sectors, by setting up a network of technology and 
livelihood -based incubators. The objective is to create new jobs and reduce rural poverty by 
encoura ging innovative micro & small enterprises in rural areas.  It seeks to tap local potential in 
farm, off -farm and agro -processing sectors and support startups/ventures that can add value to 
rural economy.  
Components:  
• Livelihood Business Incubators (LBI):  to incubate skill -based small businesses that can 
generate livelihood (for example, food processing, carpentry, handicrafts, local service 
businesses). These do not necessarily require innovation but fill local needs and create 
employment.  
• Technology Business Incubators (TBI) under ASPIRE:  focused on developing and 
nurturing ventures in agro -technology, food tech, biotech, and rural engineering fields.  
• Seed Fund for Agribusiness Startups:  provision for funding deserving startups emerging 
from these incubators.  
Eligibility:  
• Organizations (NGOs, institutions, etc.) can apply to establish LBIs or TBIs under 
ASPIRE. Often, existing incubation centers in rural or agri universities are considered.  
• Entrepreneurs/startups: primarily those from rural background or working on rural -centric 
business ideas can join these incubators or avail the scheme’s seed funding. Typically, they 
should have an innovative project in areas like agro -processing, farm mec hanization, dairy, 
poultry, fisheries, handicrafts, etc.  
Support Offered:  
• To Incubators:  One-time grant up to ₹1 crore  for setting up a Livelihood Business 
Incubator , and up to ₹1.5 – 2 crores  for a Technology Business Incubator  (especially if 
there is a focus on specific areas like agro, textile, etc. ). This money is used for capital 
expenditure (machines for training), operational costs and carrying out incubation 
programs.  
• To Startups (via Incubators):  Seed Capital Funding : Each ASPIRE TBI can receive a 
fund (e.g., ₹30 lakh) to disburse as seed loans or grants to startups they incubate. Startups 
may get an initial funding support of up to ₹5 lakh  (in the form of soft loan or grant) to 
commercialize their idea.  
• Training/Mentorship:  Incubators under ASPIRE conduct training programs to skill rural 
youth in entrepreneurship. They provide mentorship on enterprise creation, business plan, 
marketing, and technical know -how.  
• Synergy with other schemes: Enterprises emerging from ASPIRE incubators can also avail

Prime Minister’s Employment Generation Programme (PMEGP)  
Purpose & Objectives:  PMEGP is a flagship credit -linked subsidy program launched in 2008 
(and ongoing) to generate self -employment opportunities through micro -enterprise creation  
in non -farm sector. It is not startup -specific in the tech sense, but it supports any individual to start 
a new micro business  (manufacturing, processing, trading, service) by providing a subsidy on the 
bank loan.  The objective is to create sustainable employment in rural and urban areas by 
empowering youth, artisans, and unemploye d persons to become entrepreneurs.  
Eligibility:  Any Indian citizen above 18 years of age can apply to set up a new project under 
PMEGP. Educational qualification: for projects above ₹10 lakh in manufacturing or ₹5 lakh in 
services, at least VIII standard pass is required. Only new projects are consider ed (existing units 
or someone who already availed government subsidy under other schemes is not eligible). Self -
Help Groups and charitable trusts can also apply. The applicant should have a viable business idea 
and be able to invest 5 -10% of th e project cost from personal sources (margin money).  
Support Offered:  Financial – Capital Subsidy:  The scheme provides a subsidy on the project 
cost (which effectively does not have to be repaid) while the rest is a bank loan. The typical pattern:

• For general category:  Government provides 15% of project cost in urban areas and 25% 
in rural areas as subsidy.  
• For special category (SC/ST/OBC/Women/Physically disabled/NER/Hill areas):  25% 
subsidy in urban, 35% in rural.  The maximum project cost  allowed is ₹25 lakh for 
manufacturing and ₹10 lakh for service/ trading.  The beneficiary must contribute 5% 
(special category) or 10% (general) as own equity, and the rest is a bank loan. For example, 
a young entrepreneur in a rural area (general category) wants to start a small manufacturing 
unit of cost ₹20 lakh. They contri bute ₹2 lakh (10%), bank loans ₹15 lakh, and government 
gives ₹3 lakh (15%) as subsidy. If SC category in rural, subsidy would be ₹7 lakh (35%), 
etc. 
Besides subsidy:  the scheme also supports entrepreneurs with training  (an Entrepreneurship 
Development Program 10 -day training is mandatory before disbursement), and handholding by 
implementing agencies.  
Implementing Agencies:  At the national level, KVIC (Khadi and Village Industries 
Commission)  is the nodal agency. At state level, KVIC Directorates, State KVIBs (Khadi and 
Village Industries Boards), and District Industries Centres (DICs) coordinate. Banks play the 
critical role of credit appraisal and loan sanction.  
Application Procedure:  Applications are submitted online on the PMEGP e -portal. The applicant 
provides details of the project (cost, machinery, marketing plan, etc.). It is then scrutinized by the 
district -level Task Force which conducts an interview of the candidate. Recommended 
applications go to banks for loan appraisal. Once the bank sanctions the loan, it requests the subsidy 
from KVIC. After the entrepreneur completes the mandatory EDP training, the l oan and subsidy 
are disbursed so the project can st art. The process from application to sanction can take a few 
months. Margin money subsidy is deposited upfront in a blocked account and adjusted after 2 
years of successful operation (meaning if the unit runs for 2 years, subsidy becomes grant).  
Nodal Agency:  Ministry of MSME , implemented through KVIC  in collaboration with Banks  
and State DICs . 
Operational Status:  Active and expanded . PMEGP has been continually extended due to its role 
in job creation. From 2008 to 2023, it assisted roughly 7.8 lakh micro enterprises with subsidies 
disbursed over ₹19,000 crore, creating an estimated 64 lakh jobs. In recent years, targets were 
increased  (around 80,000 new units to be setup annually). The scheme is very much ongoing in 
2025 with the government allocating fresh funds each year. Recently, special focus has been given 
to support entrepreneurship among youth, women and backward classes through higher subsidies 
and easier processes.  
Official References:  The scheme details are on the KVIC website and the official PMEGP portal. 
Press releases by PIB and MSME Ministry report yearly achievements and budget outlays.  
Credit Guarantee Fund Trust for Micro & Small Enterprises (CGTMSE)

Purpose & Objectives:  Launched in 2000, CGTMSE provides collateral -free credit guarantee  
for loans to micro and small enterprises (including startups) so that banks can lend without 
collateral security. The objective is to improve credit flow to the MSE sector by securing the lender 
against default, thereby encouraging lending to new and firs t-generation entrepreneurs who lack 
collateral.  
Eligibility:  All new and existing micro & small enterprises (defined by MSME criteria) engaged 
in manufacturing or services (excluding some retail, education, etc.) are eligible for coverage. Both 
term loans and working capital facilities up to ₹200 lakh (₹2 Cr) are e ligible for guarantee. The 
borrower must meet the lender’s creditworthiness criteria,  and the loan must be sanctioned by a 
member lending institution (banks, certain NBFCs, Small Finance Banks that have registered with 
CGTMSE).  
Support Offered:  Guarantee Coverage:  Typically,  up to 75% of the loan amount  is guaranteed 
by CGTMSE (85% for micro enterprises up to ₹5 lakh, and 80% for loans to women, SC/ST, etc. 
or in Northeast region ). The maximum guarantee cover is ₹1.5 Cr for regular MSEs (recently 
increased to ₹2 Cr). The guarantee covers default in repayment – if the borrower defaults, 
CGTMSE pays the lender 75 -85% of the outstanding amount (after proceeding with recoveries). 
This s ignificantly reduces the risk for lenders and thu s motivates them to give collateral -free loans. 
Lenders pay a guarantee fee  (around 1 -1.5% of loan amount) and an annual service fee (~0.75 -
1%). Often this cost is passed to the borrower as a slightly higher interest rate. But for the 
entrepreneur, it means they can get a loan without providing any security.  
Application Procedure:  The entrepreneur doesn’t directly interact with CGTMSE; they apply 
for a loan  at a bank or financial institution and request it to be covered under CGTMSE. The bank, 
if it finds the project viable, sanctions the loan and applies to CGTMSE for coverage. The trust 
issues a guarantee cover to the lender. Essentially, from the entrepre neur’s perspective, one should 
approach a bank that is member of CGTMSE (most public sector and many private banks are), and 
mention willingness for a CGTMS E-backed loan. The rest of the process is internal between bank 
and CGTMSE.  
Nodal Agency:  CGTMSE  is a trust jointly set up by Ministry of MSME and SIDBI. It operates 
the scheme. The Ministry provides corpus contributions to the trust.  
Operational Status:  Active (scaled up in 2023).  As of 2025, CGTMSE is robust – in fact, the 
government infused ₹9,000 Cr into CGTMSE corpus in FY 2023 to dramatically expand 
guarantees for MSMEs.  This enabled reduction in fees and increase of coverage limit to ₹2 Cr. 
Over 1.6 crore guarantees have been approved since inception, supporting over ₹8 lakh crore of 
credit. Startups that qualify as MSMEs often use CGTMSE to secure working capital or equ ipment 
loans, although since 2022 they also have the option o f CGSS (which is specifically for DPIIT 
startups). Both schemes can co -exist; lenders choose based on borrower profile. CGTMSE remains 
the primary avenue for loans up to ₹2 Cr for new small businesses.  
Official References:  CGTMSE official website (cgtmse.in) provides operational details, annual 
reports show statistics. MSME Ministry press releases and PIB notes on the infusion and changes 
(for example, MSME Day announcements in 2023) highlight the enhancements.

BioNEST – Bioincubators  
Purpose & Objectives:  BioNEST supports creation of dedicated biotech incubators across India 
to provide startups with lab facilities and a conducive ecosystem.  Biotech ventures often need 
expensive equipment (like sequencers, fermentation units) and wet labs that individual startups 
cannot afford. BioNEST addresses this by funding incubators at universities, research institutes, 
and technology parks, thereby “ne sting” startups and giving them access to high -end infrastructure 
and mentorship.  
Support Offered:  BIRAC provides grants to the host institutions  to establish or expand bio -
incubation facilities. The grant can range from ₹1 -2 crore for smaller incubators to ₹5 -7 crore for 
larger centers, depending on proposed capacity. This covers renovation of space, purchase of lab 
equipment (like tissue culture labs, analytical instruments, cold rooms), and initial operating 
expenses. Many BioNEST incubators are equipped with domain -specific facilities – e.g., genomics 
labs, bio -fermentation pilot pla nts, medical device prototyping labs, etc.  
Services to Startups:  Startups admitted into BioNEST incubators (there are ~74 such incubators 
by 2025) receive:  
• Wet Lab Space:  benches and fume hoods in shared labs, or small dedicated lab suites.  
• Shared Equipment:  costly instruments (mass spectrometers, electron microscopes, etc.) 
available on time -share.  
• Technical Mentorship:  support from scientists and experts associated with the host 
institution.  
• Business Mentorship:  BIRAC facilitates connections to industry mentors, regulatory 
experts (critical for biotech), and investors.  
• Networking & Funding Opportunities:  Being in BioNEST often provides easier access 
to BIRAC’s other funding schemes (like BIG, SEED) and networking events (Bio -investor 
meets, competitions).  
• Services:  Some bioincubators also provide biosafety and ethical guidance, help with 
clinical trial design, etc., and office space for administrative work.  
Eligibility:  Host:  Academic or research institutes with a life sciences focus can apply to BIRAC 
to establish a BioNEST. They need to demonstrate availability of at least 2500 sq.ft. space and 
basic infrastructure, plus a plan for sustainability. Startups:  To join an existing BioNEST 
incubator, any startup or even an individual innovator in biotech can apply. Each BioNEST sets 
its admission criteria – typically an evaluation of the business idea’s scientific merit, feasibility, 
and fit with the incubator’s focus areas. Often, even very early researchers (with just an idea) are 
allowed if they need lab space to develop a proof of concept.

BIG – Biotechnology Ignition Grant  
Purpose & Objectives:  BIG is BIRAC’s flagship early -stage funding program. It aims to “ignite” 
new biotech ideas  by providing grant money for proving concepts and developing prototypes. The 
goal is to encourage scientists, researchers and young companies to take the plunge into 
entrepreneurship by de -risking the initial phase of high -risk discovery with government support.  
It is often the first grant a biotech entrepreneur will seek, and it has kickstarted hundreds of biotech 
startups in India.  
Eligibility:  Individuals (scientists, graduate students, innovators) or startup companies (registered 
not more than 5 years, with at least 51% Indian ownership) are eligible.  If an individual gets the 
grant, they are expected to incorporate a company within a specified time and partner with an 
incubator to manage funds. Applicants should have an innovative idea in any discipline of life 
sciences/biotech – healthcare, drugs, va ccines, devices, diagnostics, industrial biotech, agriculture, 
ecology, etc. One i mportant criterion: the idea should have potential for commercialization in the 
long term, not just research for publication.  
Support Offered:  Grant -in-Aid of up to ₹50 lakh  (₹5 million) per project.  This is a non-dilutive, 
non-repayable grant . It covers a project duration of up to 18 months to achieve proof -of-concept. 
The funds can be used for:  
• Research expenses (consumables, reagents, lab supplies).  
• Small equipment specific to the project.  
• Manpower salary (you can hire a research assistant, etc.).  
• Services like lab testing, animal studies, outsourcing certain assays.

SBIRI – Small Business Innovation Research Initiative  
Purpose & Objectives:  SBIRI, started in 2005 (and later brought under BIRAC), was one of the 
first Govt schemes to support industrial R&D in biotech. It encourages small and medium biotech 
companies  to take up risky innovative research with a potential for commercialization. The focus 
is on proof of concept and early development  of novel products or processes by providing partial 
government support so that the financial burden is reduced.

Eligibility:  Indian companies, specifically MSME or startup biotech companies  (as well as 
startups in partnership with academic collaborators) are eligible. Unlike BIG which caters to very 
early stage, SBIRI typically expects the applicant to be a registered company with some R&D 
capability. Single or consortia of companies can app ly, and collaborations with research institutes 
are encouraged. There are usually two phases: Phase I (up to ₹50L for early -stage exploratory 
work) and Phase II (up to ₹100L+ for later-stage development, scale -up, etc.). The company must 
have a sizable ownership by Indian citizens and should not be a large company (the “Small 
Business” emphasis).  
Support Offered:  Funding is provided as a mix of grant and loan.  
• For Phase I projects (exploratory stage), BIRAC may give up to 80% of the project cost as 
grant  (non-repayable) especially for very small companies.  
• For Phase II or larger projects, BIRAC might give 50% as grant and 50% as soft loan  (with 
~2-3% interest) to the company. The loan portion is repayable after the project outcome, 
with a moratorium period.  
• The total funding can go up to a few crores for successful projects (the scheme has 
flexibility based on project needs). Thus SBIRI helps companies undertake expensive R&D 
(like candidate drug development, large animal trials, prototype tooling, etc.) by s haring 
costs. The company is expected to fund the remainder through its own or other funds. The 
unique aspect is government picks up part of the tab of failed projects  (grant portion 
doesn’t need to be returned even if project fails), thereby reducing the risk for the 
enterprise.  
Application Procedure:  BIRAC issues calls for proposals  for SBIRI periodically (usually a 
couple of times a year). Proposals are submitted online detailing the project plan, team, budget, 
and business opportunity. There’s a two -tier evaluation: a Technical Evaluation Panel  of experts 
reviews the scientific merit, and a Project Approval Committee  considers the funding and 
strategic fit. Often the process has an Expression of Interest (EOI) step followed by full proposal 
from shortlisted EOIs. If approved, a Grant -in-aid cum Loan Agreement is signed between BIRAC 
and the company. Funds are released in installments tied to milestones.  
Nodal Agency:  BIRAC (DBT)  – BIRAC manages SBIRI, including selection, fund disbursement 
and monitoring. They may appoint Project Monitoring Committees to oversee progress of each 
funded project.  
Operational Status:  Active.  SBIRI has been ongoing, though in recent years BIRAC launched 
newer schemes too. Many companies that outgrew BIG or started directly with SBIRI have been 
beneficiaries. For instance, Bharat Biotech, Serum Institute, and several pharma/med -tech startups 
received SBIRI support in early stages. As of 2025, SBIRI calls are still announced under the 
BIRAC umbrella (often under the “BIRAC Innovators” program calls) . A recent call (under an 
Inclusive Innovation program) was open through  May 2025,  showing SBIRI’s continuity. The 
scheme was pivotal during COVID as well for funding diagnostics and vaccine related 
innovations.

BIPP – Biotechnology Industry Partnership Programme  
Purpose & Objectives:  BIPP is a public -private partnership scheme  that supports high -end, 
pathbreaking research  in futuristic biotech leads, through cost -sharing with industry.  The aim is 
to foster development of advanced biotechnologies  that have large -scale impact (national or 
global) and intellectual property creation in India, by reducing the financial risks for companies 
working on them. BIPP typically targets bigger or riskier projects  than SBIRI – e.g., novel 
vaccine development, transgenic crops, bi otherapeutics, platform technologies.  
Eligibility:  Industrial organizations  (startup, SME or even large companies) can apply, 
especially when in collaboration with academia  or other research entities. BIPP was open to all, 
but practically many applicants have been more established startups or SMEs given the scale. The 
project should involve an innovative breakthrough  (not incremental) and align with national 
priorities (like new vaccines, drought -resistant crops, etc.). Multi -institutional collaborations are 
encouraged due to the complexity of projects.  
Support Offered:  Equity or Grant + Loan funding  on a cost-sharing basis : 
• The government (DBT/BIRAC) can provide up to 50% of the project cost as grant  for 
the research components, especially those with high risk of failure or long gestation.  
• A part of support may also come as a conditional loan  or equity funding , particularly for 
expenses that lead to asset creation or later stage validation. The terms (interest, royalty, 
etc.) are negotiated per project.  
• There is no upper limit  per se, but projects often have budgets in range of ₹1 –3 Cr from 
BIRAC and similar from company side (so total project maybe ₹2 –6 Cr).  
• Areas like vaccines for infectious diseases, stem cell therapies, biopharma, medical 
devices, agri -biotech, climate -resilient crops, etc., have been funded.  
A key differentiator:  BIPP often funds projects that other investors shy away from because of 
uncertainty, but which, if successful, can be game -changers. BIRAC expects a royalty or revenue 
share from successful projects (in some cases) to plow back into the fund.  
Application Procedure:  BIRAC announces thematic calls  or open calls for BIPP a few times a 
year. Application is via detailed proposal. Since these are large projects, the evaluation involves:  
• Initial review by sector -specific Expert Groups , 
• Presentation by the company to a Technical Screening Committee , 
• Final approval by BIRAC/DBT’s Investment Committee  or PAC . Once approved, a 
contract is drawn specifying each party’s contributions, milestones, and financial terms. 
The project is monitored by a Project Monitoring Committee with scientific and business 
experts. Disbursements are milestone -based.

class incubators  that nurture startups in various sectors. Launched in 2016, the objective is to 
provide startups high -quality incubation —physical infra, technical facilities, experts mentorship, 
access to funding —such that they can become scalable and sustainable businesses.  By establishing 
these in universities and institutions across India, AIM hopes to decentralize innovation support.  
Support Offered (to Host & Startups):

Atal Community Innovation Centres (ACIC)  
Purpose & Objectives:  ACIC was launched in 2019 under AIM to promote innovation and 
entrepreneurship in unserved & underserved regions  – i.e., communities beyond the major urban 
areas, such as tier -2/tier -3 cities, rural areas, and backward districts. The idea is to establish centers 
that can act as hubs for ideation, innovation and startup incubation relevant to local needs (like 
rural healthcare, crafts, agriculture technology for local farmers, etc.). ACICs involve the local 
community in problem -solving and venture creation.  
Support Offered:  AIM provides a grant up to ₹2.5 crore  (with a matching contribution required 
from the host) to set up each ACIC. This grant is used over a period (usually 2 years establishment 
+ 3 years operation) to:  
• Develop a community innovation lab space accessible to local innovators (could be at a 
college, panchayat building, etc.).  
• Purchase tools and equipment relevant to local innovation (e.g., woodworking tools if 
focusing on carpentry innovation, small prototyping tools, internet and computer facilities).  
• Run outreach programs like innovation challenges, bootcamps, and toy labs for school 
dropouts or youth.  
• Offer pre -incubation and mentorship to take grassroots ideas towards pilot stage. ACICs 
are more flexible and grassroots -oriented than AICs. They might support not only tech 
startups but also social enterprises, micro -entrepreneurs, or even NGOs with innov ative 
solutions.  
Eligibility (Host):  Universities, engineering colleges, nonprofit organizations, rural development 
institutions, or even industry in backward areas can apply to host an ACIC. They must show strong 
community connects and some innovation focus. They also need to bring at least  25-30% of the 
project cost (land, building, staff, or cash). Preference is given to aspirational districts (as identified 
by NITI) and regions with no existing incubation infrastructure.  
Application Procedure:  AIM issues separate calls for ACIC proposals. The application involves 
concept note submission and later a detailed proposal. Assessment looks at the approach to involve 
local community, sectors of focus, sustainability plan (ACICs may generate revenue vi a training 
programs or local industry support). Dozens of ACICs were approved in first rounds.  
For Innovators/Startups:  ACICs engage with the community to source ideas – via hackathons, 
community meetings, school programs, etc. Anyone from the local area with an innovation or 
business idea can approach the ACIC. The ACIC team will evaluate and, if feasible, assist them 
through product development, connecting to technical experts (often via the nearest AIC or 
technical institute), and eventually help them either form a startup or license the innovation to a

local entrepreneur. Some ACICs provide micro -grants or help arrange funding through CSR 
sources.  
Nodal Agency:  NITI Aayog (AIM) , similar governance to AIC but a distinct program.  
Operational Status:  Active.  The first cohort of ACICs (about 14 centers) were approved in 2020. 
By 2022, around 50 ACICs had been planned across various states. They are at different stages – 
some fully operational (running programs with local schools, etc.), others in setup. Exampl es

Atal New India Challenge (ANIC) and ARISE  
Purpose & Objectives:  ANIC  is a series of innovation challenges  launched by AIM in 
partnership with various ministries to find and support novel product solutions for specific 
national problems . Startups and MSMEs are invited to propose solutions to challenge statements 
(e.g., affordable solar -powered cold storage, or portable water quality testing). Winners receive 
grants to develop and deploy their product. ARISE (Applied Research and Innovation for Small 
Enterprises)  is a similar program focusing on MSME s solving challenges posed by public sector 
and defense  (run with ISRO/DRDO etc.).  
The objective is to direct India’s innovative startups towards priority areas of national interest by 
offering them non -dilutive capital and pilot opportunities. It also helps government agencies scout 
cutting -edge solutions from startups.  
Eligibility:  Indian startups, MSMEs, and individual innovators (with capability to productize) can 
participate. Typically, the applicant should have at least a prototype or detailed concept to address 
the challenge. The solutions need to be original and technically feasible . Some challenges require 
field testing or manufacturing capabilities, so consortium bids (startup + research lab, etc.) are 
allowed.  
Support Offered:  Each ANIC call has multiple winners (for different challenge areas). Grant 
support up to ₹1 crore  is provided to each winning solution to develop the product to deployment 
stage.  Milestones are defined (proof of concept, product development, pilot deployment) and grant 
tranches released accordingly. Additionally, winners often get:  
• Mentorship from sector experts (e.g., if challenge was in agriculture, ICAR scientists might 
mentor).  
• Opportunity for pilot in a govt setting  (e.g., if the solution is for clean water, government 
facilitates testing in a village or facility).

• Potential fast -track procurement or recommendation to scale if the pilot succeeds (though 
not guaranteed, it often leads to more orders from government or at least publicity). ARISE, 
similarly, might provide grants or co -fund with participating agency and give opportunity 
to integrate the solution into their systems (like a defense lab or ISRO testing it).  
Application Procedure:  Challenges are announced on the AIM website and Startup India portal. 
Each challenge statement includes problem description, expectations, and judging criteria. 
Innovators submit an online proposal specific to that challenge. A panel of technical and indu stry 
experts evaluates proposals. Top applicants are often invited to present (pitch or demonstration). 
Finally, 1 or more winners per challenge are selected for funding. The entire cycle from challenge 
launch to winner selection can be ~6 months. For example, ANIC 1.0 had 24 challenges in areas 
like Affordable Healthcare, Housing, Food Processing etc., and selected 30+ winners.

timeline is usually 12 -18 months to deliver results. Monitoring committees track progress.  
Nodal Agency:  Atal Innovation Mission, NITI Aayog , in collaboration with the line ministry 
or department  which sponsored the challenge problem (like Ministry of Housing for a housing 
tech challenge, etc.).  
Operational Status:  Active.  ANIC 1.0 was launched in 2018, ANIC 2.0 in 2021 with new set of 
challenges (including post -Covid problems), and ANIC 3.0 in 2023. So it’s an ongoing series. 
Many innovative products have come out: e.g., low -cost robotic prosthetics, AI -based crop pest 
detection, etc. ARISE has similarly run defense and space challenges (some coordinated with 
iDEX of Defence Ministry). In 2025, expect more challenges aligning with climate tech, energy, 
AI, etc., as priorities evolve.

Purpose & Objectives:  Launched in 2018 by NITI Aayog, WEP  is a unified portal to bring 
together women entrepreneurs  and ecosystem partners. Its vision is to empower women 
entrepreneurs  by providing them easy access to all relevant resources – not by creating new 
schemes, but by aggregating information, networks, and offerings in one place.  WEP operates 
under three pillars: Iccha Shakti (motivating aspiring entrepreneurs), Gyan Shakti (providing 
knowledge & ecosystem support), and Karma Shakti (providing hands -on support to wome n in 
setting up enterprises).  
Features and Support:  
• Aggregated Schemes & Services:  WEP lists and links all major schemes aimed at 
women  by various central ministries (e.g., Mudra’s women -focused Mahila Uddyami

• Skilling and Mentorship:  The platform partners with organizations to offer free courses,

tailored for women. It also has a Mentor Connect where experienced women entrepreneurs 
or professionals guide newer founders.  
• Community & Networking:  WEP provides an online community for women founders to 
connect, share stories, and find collaboration. It regularly features success stories , which

inked a partnership in 2022 with the United Nations Development  Program to further extend its

Animal Husbandry Startup and Infrastructure Support  
Animal Husbandry (AH) and Dairying also face challenges ripe for innovation – e.g., improving 
milk yield, better feed, disease diagnostics for cattle, value -added dairy products. The ministry has 
approached this via both hackathon -style challenges  and a major infrastructure funding 
scheme (AHIDF) : 
Animal Husbandry Startup Grand Challenge:  
• Initiated in 2019 and a second edition in 2021, in collaboration with Startup India.  
• Similar to fisheries challenge: identified problem statements like cost -effective cold chain 
for vaccines, breed improvement solutions, manure management, etc.  
• Startups were invited to submit solutions. Winning ideas (usually 5 -6 winners) received 
cash grants (₹10 lakh for 1st prize, ₹7 lakh 2nd, ₹5 lakh 3rd, etc.), and incubation offers at 
renowned agri incubators (e.g., at IITs or IVRI Bareilly).  
• It helped surface startups like “Stellapps” (for dairy supply chain) in earlier days or a 
startup making livestock wearable devices.  
• Status:  These challenges helped shape policy but are ad -hoc events (not continual scheme). 
The Ministry indicated it would support implementing worthwhile solutions from these 
winners across its programs.  
Animal Husbandry Infrastructure Development Fund (AHIDF):  
• Launched as part of Atmanirbhar Bharat in 2020. Size ₹15,000 Cr.  
• Purpose:  Provide concessional financing for private sector to establish infrastructure in 
dairy, meat processing, and animal feed segments.  
• Eligibility:  Any private company, FPO, Section -8, MSME, or startup in relevant sector.  
• Support:  Interest subvention of 3%  on loans (above base rate) for eligible projects.  Loan 
duration up to 8 years with 2 -year moratorium. The loan can cover 90% of project cost. 
Credit guarantee  by NABARD for up to 25% of loan (for certain borrowers) to encourage 
banks.  
• This effectively lowers the cost of capital significantly. Example: a ₹10 Cr loan at 9% 
interest becomes 6% with subvention.  
• Typical activities: new milk processing plants, meat processing facilities, breed 
improvement tech centers, livestock vaccines/drugs production units, or even setting up 
retail chains for milk/meat.  
• Application:  Via an online portal to the Dept of Animal Husbandry which forwards to 
banks or  directly banks which coordinate sanction with NABARD and DAHD.  
• Status: Operational. Implementation is via participating banks/financial institutions with 
interest subvention and other terms as per DAHD/NABARD guidelines. For the latest 
validity window, eligible activities, and current financial terms, refer to the mos t recent 
AHIDF operational guidelines/portal.

iDEX (Innovations for Defence Excellence)  
Purpose & Objectives:  Launched in 2018 under the Department of Defence Production, iDEX 
aims to create an ecosystem where startups and MSMEs innovate solutions for Indian 
defence requirements . The idea is to leverage the ingenuity and speed of startups to solve 
operational challenges of the armed forces and reduce import dependence. iDEX acts as a bridge 
between the user (Army, Navy, AirForce, DRDO labs) and the innovators (startups, individual  
innovators, MSMEs).  
How iDEX Works:  

--- Central Government Schemes for Startups - Updated as of 30Jan2026 (1).pdf Page 48 ---
• It issues problem statements as challenges  in areas like drones, cybersecurity, AI, 
robotics, mobility, maritime systems, etc. (sourced from armed forces/DRDO needs).  
• Startups propose solutions. Through a competitive process, the most promising proposals 
are selected for funding and incubation.  
• This happens in cohorts called Defence India Startup Challenges (DISC)  or through 
Open Challenges  for broader concepts.  
Support Offered:  
• Grant Funding (Prototype Development Fund):  Winners under iDEX (termed iDEX 
“innovators”) receive grants of up to ₹1.5 crore  to develop a prototype of their solution.  
This is typically non -equity, milestone -based funding from the Defence Innovation 
Organisation (the implementing body for iDEX). If two phases are needed, Phase 1 smaller 
grant for PoC, and Phase 2 larger for prototype.  
• Incubation & Mentoring:  Innovators are attached to a relevant Partner Incubator  (iDEX 
has partnered incubators across India, often IITs or tech parks) who assist in product 
development, provide workspace, and guide the startup in navigating defence 
requirements.  
• Testing & Co -Development:  The startups get access to military test facilities or DRDO 
labs to test their prototypes. They also are guided by ‘SPOCs’ (Single Point of Contact) 
from services  who help refine the solution to actual military context.  
• Commercialization Pathway:  If the prototype is successful and meets user’s needs, the 
armed forces can induct it via the Make -II procurement route  or other fast -track 
mechanisms. Essentially, the startup can get a supply order or further funding for bulk 
production, making the project commercially viable. iDEX innovation that succeed thus 
have MoD as the first buyer.  
• Recognition & Market access:  Being an iDEX winner lends credibility, making it easier 
to get private investment. Some even attract interest for exports. MoD often showcases 
iDEX products in defence expos, giving marketing push.  
Eligibility:  Indian startups (as per DPIIT definition), Indian MSMEs (as per MSME definition), 
and even individual innovators (including students or researchers, though typically they’d need to 
partner with an entity for execution) are eligible to apply. Foreign compa nies or Indian subsidiaries 
of foreign defense firms are not eligible – it’s aimed at domestic talent.  
Application Procedure:  
• Challenges:  MoD publishes challenge statements (e.g., “See Through Armor for tanks” or 
“High altitude autonomous tethered drone”). This is done via the iDEX website and Startup 
India portal.  
• Startups apply online with details of their proposed solution, team, and feasibility.  
• A screening committee shortlists entries, followed by presentations or pitch sessions.  
• Selection committee (including officers and domain experts) chooses winners for each 
challenge.  
• Thereafter, a tri -partite contract is signed among the startup, the Defence Innovation 
Organisation (DIO), and the incubator.

• The project execution time is usually 12 -18 months to deliver prototype.  
Nodal Agency:  Defence Innovation Organisation (DIO)  – a Section 8 company set up jointly 
by Defence PSUs HAL and BEL under MoD – implements iDEX. It is supported by the iDEX 
team under Department of Defence Production. Overall supervision by the Defence Innovation 
Council  chaired by MoD’s Secretary (Defence Production) and includes representatives from the 
armed services.  
Operational Status:  Active and expanding . As of 2025, iDEX has launched several rounds: 
DISC 1 to DISC 7, plus Open Challenges, totalling 100+ problem statements . Over 200 startups  
have been funded.  Many prototypes (like unmanned supply drone for Army, AI -based satellite 
imagery tools) have been delivered; a few are in process of induction. The Union Budget 2022 
allocated ₹300 Cr for iDEX, signalling high priority. In 2023, MoD set a goal to procure at least 
25 iDEX products. iDEX has also been opened to ci vilian applications for internal security (in 
collaboration with CAPFs). It continues to receive new problem statements regularly from 
Services.  
Official References:  The iDEX official website (idex.gov.in) lists challenges and awardees. Press 
releases from Press Information Bureau on each DISC iteration give details on funding and 
winners.  The Department of Defence Production’s annual reports also outline iDEX achievements.  
Technology Development Fund (TDF by DRDO)  
Purpose & Objectives:  TDF was launched in 2016 by DRDO to involve Indian industry, 
particularly startups and SMEs, in defence technology development for futuristic needs. It provides 
funding for development of technologies or prototypes that fill technological gaps  in defence 
requirements and where industries are not investing due to high risk or long gestation. It aligns 
with the indigenization goals – reducing imports by developing that tech in -house.  
Eligibility:  Indian companies classified as MSME or startups can apply, either singly or as a 
consortium (with another industry or academia). They must have majority Indian ownership. They 
should demonstrate capability to carry out R&D in the proposed field (or tie -up with a research 
institution). The project result should have potential use by Indian military or in defence sector 
exports.  
Support Offered:  
• Funding:  TDF provides up to 90% of the total project cost  as a grant -in-aid (the 
company contributes at least 10% ). Maximum funding per project is ₹10 crore . So, for a 
project budget ₹11 Cr, DRDO could fund ₹9.9 Cr, company puts ₹1.1 Cr.  
• Duration:  Projects are typically 2 years (can vary based on complexity).  
• Areas:  Electronics, armaments, avionics, naval systems, materials, robotics, etc. – often 
aligned to DRDO’s tech roadmap or services’ needs.  
• DRDO Support:  The company can consult relevant DRDO labs for technical guidance. 
DRDO may also facilitate testing of developed systems in its ranges or facilities. However, 

--- Central Government Schemes for Startups - Updated as of 30Jan2026 (1).pdf Page 50 ---
unlike iDEX, TDF projects are often more upstream tech (e.g. new materials, components) 
rather than immediate deployment systems.  
• If the project is successful, DRDO might further engage the company for production or 
integrate the tech into bigger systems.  
Application Procedure:  
• DRDO periodically publishes problem statements or desired technologies  for which it 
invites proposals under TDF. Sometimes it’s an open call where industry can propose any 
technology that fits broad themes.  
• Companies submit an Expression of Interest / Proposal outlining the technical approach, 
team, budget.  
• Proposals are evaluated by a TDF committee of scientists and experts. Criteria include 
innovation level, technical feasibility, industrial capability, cost.  
• Once approved, a MoU/Agreement is signed. Project oversight is by a monitoring team 
from DRDO.  
• Funds are released in phases (e.g., 20% on signing, then based on milestone reviews).  
Nodal Agency:  Defence Research & Development Organisation (DRDO) , under MoD. 
Specifically managed by DRDO’s Directorate of Industry Interface (or similar). DRDO has tied 
up with PSA -IL (Project Sanctioning Authority - Indian industry and startups Liaison)  to 
promote it.  
Operational Status:  Active.  TDF got a boost with budget 2022 doubling its allocation. By mid -
2023, about 50 projects had been awarded under TDF. Examples: development of radar 
components by an SME, advanced optical sights by a startup, new propellant chemical by a small 
firm. Many o f these are in prototype/testing stage. The program is ongoing with new calls – 
recently DRDO listed needs in AI and autonomous systems for TDF. Startups with deep -tech (like 
new materials, sensors, etc.) are increasingly applyin g. MoD envisions more and more DRDO tech 
development via such industry partnerships.  
Official References:  DRDO’s official TDF portal (tdf.drdo.gov.in) has scheme guidelines and list 
of approved projects (to the extent not classified). Press releases from MOD and statements in 
Parliament (Lok Sabha starred questions) also give data on number of projects sancti oned, etc. 
(e.g., in a LS Q&A in 2022, government stated TDF projects count and budget).
